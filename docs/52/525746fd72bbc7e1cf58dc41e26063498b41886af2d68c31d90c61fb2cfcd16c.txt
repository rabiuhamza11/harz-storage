Share Advertisement Nigeria should resist calls to restore broad petrol subsidies and instead channel relief to vulnerable households, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) has warned. The IMF said that restoring fuel subsidies are costly, disproportionately benefit higher-income consumers and risk worsening fiscal pressures amid rising borrowing costs. Fuel subsidies or measures preventing prices from adjusting fully to an energy shock had cost countries between 1 percent and 1.5 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) in 2026, Rodrigo Valdés, director of the IMF’s fiscal affairs department, said on the sidelines of the IMF and World Bank meeting in Bangkok, Thailand, on Wednesday. “Protecting people is not the same as protecting the price of fuels,” Valdés said. “It’s first fiscally very costly, and second, if you look at the pattern of consumption, it’s easy to see that that subsidy is readily regressive in many places,” he added. Valdés made the comments following a question on how the Washington-based fund could recommend allowing energy prices to reflect scarcity when higher fuel prices in Nigeria could raise transport costs, worsen inflation and deepen poverty, particularly when the government was facing calls to restore petrol subsidy ahead of an election year. The IMF director urged governments to improve social safety nets and household data to identify people who need assistance rather than re-introducing fuel subsidies that are costly and unsustainable. Nigeria recently introduced a 30-day petrol discount at state-owned oil company, Nigerian National Petroleum Company (NNPC) Limited retail stations, with priority for public transport operators, in a bid to bring respite to transporters against rising fuel prices. The petrol cap, though Taiwo Oyedele, minister of finance, explained that the NNPCL would forego its retail profit margin to absorb the difference, analysts and economists have widely argued that it is a “back door” subsidy, especially as Africa’s most populous nation faces a crunch election in less than 100 days. Leading opposition figures including former vice president Atiku Abubakar, the presidential candidate for the African Democratic Congress (ADC) and former Anambra governor Peter Obi, the presidential candidate of the National Democratic Congress (NDC) have equally vowed to restore petrol subsidies and fight corruption in what experts say could hurt the Nigerian economy more and dissipate the gains of the hard won reforms, including a now stable naira, cooling inflation and quickening growth. Davide Furceri, a division chief in the fund’s fiscal affairs department, said governments could redirect some of the resources spent on blanket subsidies towards vulnerable consumers. “For example, you mentioned transportation costs. Yeah, gasoline could go up, and perhaps bus fees may be subsidised in a way that is a much better policy at the end of the day than just a blanket subsidy for everybody,” Furceri said. He also suggested alternatives such as school feeding programmes, geographical targeting and assistance for vulnerable farmers. Furceri acknowledged that some countries lacked household registries and digital payment systems needed to deliver targeted support effectively. However, he said governments could adopt alternative approaches in the short term while developing stronger systems. He recommended that assistance should be limited, targeted and temporary. Furceri explained that fuel subsidies could worsen global energy pressures by preventing demand from adjusting when supplies were constrained. “When you subsidise, you don’t allow, at the end of the day, demand to adjust,” he said. “And the reality is that we have less fuels in the world economy. That means that someone else has to adjust more, and the market produces a higher price for that.” The IMF official said subsidies were not only costly and regressive but could also have spillover effects on the global economy.
