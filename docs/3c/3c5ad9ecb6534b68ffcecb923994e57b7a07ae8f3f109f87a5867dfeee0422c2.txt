But the reality is that partisan control on Capitol Hill will be determined by just a handful of competitive House and Senate contests. The Cook Political Report currently classifies only [22 House campaigns](https://www.cookpolitical.com/ratings/house-race-ratings) and only [seven Senate campaigns](https://www.cookpolitical.com/ratings/senate-race-ratings) as toss-ups, where either the Democrat or the Republican has a genuine chance of winning. By definition, neither party enjoys a clear advantage in these contests, so [both will look](https://thehill.com/homenews/senate/6124725-trump-senate-race-risk/) to augment their base support with [the votes of independents](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/independent-voters-shift-toward-democrats-ahead-of-us-midterms-poll/4048549). As [a political science scholar](https://umdearborn.edu/people-um-dearborn/julio-borquez) focused on voting and elections, I believe that in the last few weeks before Election Day, those voters will feature prominently in campaign strategies. Independents are dissatisfied Midterm congressional elections serve as a [referendum on the president’s performance](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/politics-of-congressional-elections-9781538176733/). [Presidential approval](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/10/04/trump-approval-rating-midterms-republicans-polls/92061673007/) polls and [surveys about the economy](https://www.courthousenews.com/voters-sour-on-trump-economy-as-democrats-open-11-point-midterm-lead-in-new-polling/) indicate that independents do not favor [President Donald Trump or the Republican Party](https://www.ap.org/news-highlights/elections/2026/where-trump-has-lost-support-with-independents-according-to-ap-norc-polling/). A [September 2026 YouGov](https://d3nkl3psvxxpe9.cloudfront.net/documents/econTabReport_5WiMaRX.pdf) survey found that only 23% of independents approve of Trump’s job as president, compared with 79% of Republicans and 2% of Democrats. This continues a [steady drop in independents’ approval](https://apnorc.org/projects/trump-has-lost-support-from-independents-over-the-course-of-his-second-term/) of Trump throughout his second term. Independents have also soured on Trump’s handling of the economy, with 73% in a September Fox News poll saying that Trump has [made the economy worse](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/fox-news-poll-squeezed-costs-frustrated-voters-turn-democrats?utm_source=substack&utm_medium=email), a sentiment also expressed by 95% of Democrats and 26% of Republicans. Independents have also registered their discontent with the war in Iran. Only 22% of independents think the [war has been worth fighting](https://globalaffairs.org/research/public-opinion-survey/7-10-americans-say-iran-war-not-worth-fighting), a view expressed by 63% of Republicans and 8% of Democrats. And 83% of independents [do not think the Trump administration has a clear plan](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/fox-news-poll-71-think-trump-administration-lacks-plan-end-iran-war) for ending the war. Across these policy questions and performance assessments, Democrats and Republicans express clear and consistent support for their party’s positions and candidates. Independents, not surprisingly, stake out something of a middle ground. But on key predictors of vote choice, especially presidential approval and economic evaluations, the opinions of [independents appear to be closer to those of Democrats](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/democrats-lead-in-new-poll-as-trump-looms-over-midterms) than to Republicans. Independents’ current pro-Democratic slant is not dramatic, but it could be significant in crucial toss-up contests. One prominent Republican pollster, David Winston, has sounded an alarm about “[unhappy independents](https://rollcall.com/2026/06/10/unhappy-independents-will-decide-this-election/)” and their threat to continued GOP control of Congress. A recent poll shows that independents are very excited about voting in the midterms. [SDI Productions/Getty Images](https://www.gettyimages.com/detail/photo/rear-view-of-long-line-of-unrecognizable-diverse-royalty-free-image/1495396753?phrase=Independent%20voters%20US&searchscope=image%2Cfilm&adppopup=true) How to win the independent vote In closely fought House and Senate contests, both parties need to make inroads with independent voters, which poses some challenges. First, independent voters’ turnout in midterms [usually lags behind](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/politics-of-congressional-elections-9781538176733/) that of party members. So motivating and mobilizing independents will likely require added effort. It’s worth noting, however, that a recent Emerson College poll showed [70% of independents were very excited](https://emersoncollegepolling.com/september-2026-national-poll-democrats/) about voting in the midterms. That’s on par with the level of enthusiasm expressed by Republicans and Democrats. A second challenge for candidates is to develop campaign messaging that can connect with independent voters. For almost any candidate [running for office](https://www.routledge.com/The-Campaign-Manager-Running-and-Winning-Local-Elections/Shaw/p/book/9781032658322), a top priority is to [mobilize the party base](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/campaign-craft-9781440837326/). This is especially critical in toss-up contests such as the Michigan or Texas Senate races, where the base alone won’t guarantee victory. But the themes that motivate the party faithful may not sit well with independents, who tend to [recoil against partisan rancor](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/independent-politics/23F3E25D6AE3A35E94993C7D4409FA5D). For instance, Republican voters in Michigan’s 10th Congressional District might applaud Mike Bouchard’s pledge to combat “[radical socialism](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NFLAH1_lrNM),” but independent voters might find it a bit overdone. By contrast, independent voters do appear to be [responsive to the more substantive policy priorities](https://doi.org/10.1177/106591290305600404) of congressional candidates. In other words, congressional candidates, especially those in highly competitive races in [Texas](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/10/06/texas-2026-elections-competitive-races-us-congress-state-legislature/), [Nebraska](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/most-republicans-tight-midterm-distance-themselves-trump-reuters-review-finds-2026-10-07/) and [Kansas](https://www.forbes.com/sites/saradorn/2026/10/07/hottest-2026-midterm-races-historically-red-kansas-becomes-toss-up-updated-daily/?streamIndex=0), will need to employ sharply partisan rhetoric to fire up their base while also offering more sober, policy-based appeals to independents. Striking that balance might be especially difficult for Republican candidates. Trump clearly wants the midterms to be [a referendum on his record](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oco_Yf1iSoA), and he wants voters to imagine that [he is on the ballot](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/23/nx-s1-5977613/trump-poll-democrats-congress). It’s an effort by the White House to increase turnout among so-called [low-propensity Trump voters](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/trump-promised-to-campaign-in-midterm-battlegrounds-hes-starting-in-deep-red-states-instead). These are people who have voted for Trump in presidential elections and continue to support the president, but who often do not vote in midterms. Putting Trump center stage might well excite this segment of Trump supporters, but it might also remind independents of their deepening reservations about the president. Democratic congressional candidates will also need to be careful about balancing partisan messages to their base with more measured appeals to independent voters. But Democrats don’t have to share the spotlight with the president, so their balancing acts are not as delicate as those confronting their Republican opponents. At this point in the campaign, it appears that the Trump administration’s record on issues that matter to Americans is pushing independents – in some cases gently, in some cases more decidedly – in a Democratic direction.
