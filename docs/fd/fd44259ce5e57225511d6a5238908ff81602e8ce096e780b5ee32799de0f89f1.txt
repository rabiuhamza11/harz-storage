Americans feel pinch of continued Iran war: ‘It’s screwed everything up’This article is more than 2 months oldWhether in favor of or against the US-Israel war on Iran, people across the country are left holding the bill and wondering on its purposeGeorge Chidi in Marietta, Georgia, Shrai Popat in North York, Maine, Rachel Leingang in Superior, Wisconsin, and Joseph Gedeon in Washington. Graphics by Andrew WitherspoonTue 28 Jul 2026 06.00 EDTLast modified on Tue 28 Jul 2026 14.56 EDTSharePrefer the Guardian on GoogleFive months after the United States and Israel started a historically unpopular war against Iran that was going to supposedly last weeks, the fighting has refused to resolve in victory, defeat or peace. Iran’s supreme leader had been killed in the opening strikes, ceasefires broke down, and the strait of Hormuz has been closed and reopened.For voters interviewed across the country, while the damage may be distant, the residual effects have shown up at the pump, on the grocery bill and in a phone call telling a spouse they have a day to report for deployment. And for a country still absorbing the aftermath of Israel’s war on Gaza – funded to the tune of billions of dollars by the United States – the war in Iran lands on top of an already raw argument about what American power is for.In North York, Maine, Lydia Stone, 31, works in the logging industry and is raising an 18-month-old daughter. She describes an industry being squeezed from both directions: fuel costs rising while the price customers pay for wood stays flat. “It’s been a huge jump in prices, and makes life a lot harder,” she said.Trump administration’s revision of Iran war casualties ‘huge disrespect’, say veteransRead moreThat squeeze shows up in the data, with the average price of gasoline pushing above $4 a gallon for the first time in years, according to AAA. Analysts have also noted the pain is not evenly shared: lower- and middle-income households spend a larger share of their budgets on fuel, meaning the same price spike lands harder on them than on wealthier consumers.Stone’s frustration isn’t only economic. “A huge part of what has been so frustrating ... is like the fact my tax dollars are going to fund murdering people, at the same time that it’s cutting social services, it’s cutting Snap benefits for my neighbors,” she said, referring to nutrition benefits for low-income Americans.She said the line at her local food pantry had grown noticeably longer and her best friend’s husband, she said, was deployed on little notice.US economic indicators during the war in Iran chartedThat sense of a war without justification reverberated among voters who described themselves as independent or progressive. Terrence Dahmen, 73, a retired, independent-leaning voter from Cable, Wisconsin, who said he usually ends up voting Democrat, called American involvement “a huge mistake”. “We weren’t provoked into that fight, and we weren’t attacked,” he said. “I saw no reason to go there other than possibly controlling oil somehow.”He argued the country should be investing in solar, wind and geothermal power instead, and said he wished the US had taken a different path on climate policy years ago.Robert Edelstein, 62, who runs a window-tinting business in Douglas county, Wisconsin, was more blunt. “In the general election, I would automatically vote for anybody that was against the war,” he said. “I think it’s the stupidest thing that could ever possibly have happened.” Comparing the decision to strike Iran unfavorably with the Iraq war launched under George W Bush, he added: “It’s screwed everything up, as we all know ... it’s absolutely stupid.”In Georgia, Jimmy Carter – a retired teacher not related to the former president – watched Donald Trump’s motorcade arrive near his old school last week with similar disbelief. “It’s a dumbass idea,” he said. “I don’t know who fed him that idea.” He noted that even his own brother, a Trump voter, agreed.Voters with personal connections to the military comprised a wide range of views.Nick Marine, a retired sailor at a Trump rally in Marietta, offered full-throated endorsement of the Trump administration’s approach. “I think what President Trump is doing is good,” he said. “I think it took a long time for us to get to this point ... I’m glad it’s finally being resolved.”He argued for gradualism over overwhelming force – “you don’t want to behead your neighbor, because in a month or so, you’re going to be doing commerce with them” – while voicing concern for Iranian civilians cut off from water, sanitation and electricity by the fighting.Richard Bradley, a retired army major and combat engineer from Mabelton, Georgia, whose son and daughter currently serve, supports the broader rationale for confronting Iran but wants a clear exit. “I’d rather fight bad guys in their land instead of mine,” he said.View image in fullscreenRichard Bradley, a retired army major, supported the Iran war. Photograph: George Chidi/The GuardianStill, he said: “I kind of take a little bit of an issue with where we’re at now ... we need to be figuring out how to finish it up, so it doesn’t turn into another ... enduring war.” His own father fought in the Gulf war, he noted, and he didn’t “want my kids to fight unfinished business that my generation left.”Not all of the war’s critics oppose the war itself. Salman Majidi, an Iranian immigrant and software developer from Atlanta, stood among anti-Trump protesters holding a “Free Iran” sign – but for the opposite reason. A supporter of Iran’s exiled former crown prince, Reza Pahlavi, Majidi argued the American campaign was a rescue rather than a war of choice.“Tens of thousands of Iranian civilians have been murdered by the Islamic regime in just two days,” he said, arguing the toll predates and outweighs the American strikes. “We deserve a better life in Iran.”But given the proximity and relationship of the conflicts, the Iran war has not displaced the argument over Gaza so much as compounded it, blurring into a single grievance about where American money and moral authority are being spent.Clay Harvison, a mixed martial artist from Marietta who voted for the president, said the conflict should have ended months ago. “He should have left it when he had already bombed them,” Harvison said, blaming pressure from Israeli leaders for dragging the US deeper in. He said the war threatens the legacy Trump seems to prize most: “being remembered as being great ... he’s messing with that really bad right now.”Younger voters in particular, he said, were asking why a president who campaigned against foreign wars is now presiding over one, arguing that “the military-industrial complex is laundering our tax dollars instead of going into fixing our streets”.Whether a ceasefire holds, and whether Tehran and Washington ever sit down for something more than a pause, is still an open question.What is not in doubt, from Wisconsin lake towns to the Georgia suburbs, is that voters of nearly every political persuasion are doing the same math: adding up gas receipts, deployment orders and casualty counts, and asking what, precisely, the war is for.Explore more on these topicsUS newsUS-Israel war on IranTrump administrationDonald TrumpGeorgiaMaineWisconsinnewsShareReuse this content
