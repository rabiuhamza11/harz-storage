A perfectly ripe cheese can be a transcendent eating experience, but what is the best way to manage that aging window? Storing cheese improperly can negatively affect taste and texture, and can hasten the growth of bad mold. Proper storage can preserve the product for weeks and even go so far as to encourage the good, naturally occurring mold to grow back.By keeping factors such as moisture retention, breathability and flavor impact in mind, you can store cheese to keep it tasting as good as possible, for as long as possible. So is cheese storage really necessary? In short, yes, depending on your habits. If you’re looking to give your Humboldt Fog double the shelf life, then forget what you know about cheese storage and take a look at some of the products below.Why you should trust usTo gain some insight into how the world of cheese professionals store their cheese, we talked with Emilia D’Albero, ACS CCP, a member of Guilde Internationale des Fromagers and Meilleure Fromagère du Monde 2025. (Full disclosure: among her other titles, D’Albero is the sales and marketing manager at Formaticum, which is one of the brands in our roundup.) When asked about what makes a cheese storage method effective, she said: “The two key elements are breathability and humidity ... It allows the cheese to breathe because, remember, cheese is a living product. It needs oxygen … so [proper storage] allows the cheese to breathe but also maintains the right amount of humidity in the package.”We decided to dive deeper into the mechanics of cheese and spoke with Michael Tunick, PhD, an adjunct professor of food science at Drexel University, and the author of The Science of Cheese. “Cheese is a living object,” says Tunick. If it’s just stored in plastic, he explains, “eventually microbes will die out so you are not getting more flavor development. If you are going to keep it for a while … then it ages better when there is some sort of ventilation.” As cheese ages, the microbes change over time. “I just saw an article about an English cheese [that] had over a thousand species in there,” Tunick says.Not all microbes are a benefit, however. While there are many cheeses where molds are purposely introduced, for example to add flavor in blue cheese, Tunick says that if you see green or pink mold growing on the outside of your cheese, you should be wary. If it’s on a hard cheese, you can just cut off the mold and continue to eat it. With soft cheeses, the mold can grow deep inside – if you see green or pink mold growing on a wedge of camembert, for instance, play it safe and throw it away.How we tested ways to store cheeseView image in fullscreen Photograph: Matthew Schaffner/The GuardianOver the course of two months, we tested brie and cheddar in nine different storage methods. We wanted to look at how well they worked for different types of cheeses while simulating normal storage and use conditions. To minimize variables, we used samples of brie and cheddar from the same wheel and block, respectively. That’s why we included several options in our testing that many people might have already lying around their house.The best of these methods we tested was simply wrapping the cheese in a paper towel and then placing it in a resealable plastic bag, for an easy and relatively effective way to manage moisture. The sealed bag trapped the moisture evaporating from the cheese, creating a humid environment. However, if the bag is completely sealed and rarely, if ever opened, there could be problems. Cheese stored in a sealed plastic container or directly in a plastic bag saw condensation of full water droplets, which could then drip on to the cheese. This was visually unappealing and allowed the surface of the cheese to grow unwanted microbes, which led to spoilage and unpleasant smells. The additional layer of the paper towel is key – it absorbs that extra moisture without becoming completely saturated, so it helps keep the cheese humid but not wet.The other potential downside of the completely sealed plastic bag is lack of oxygen. This may have contributed to the slower rind regrowth of the brie stored in this method in comparison to the cheese paper. That said, there was still some rind showing on the cut faces.
