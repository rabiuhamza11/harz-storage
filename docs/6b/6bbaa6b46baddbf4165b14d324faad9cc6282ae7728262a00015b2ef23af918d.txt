[Margi Rentis/Florida International University](https://news.fiu.edu/2026/fius-wall-of-wind-tests-manufactured-home-across-various-u.s-installation-practices) We blasted mobile homes with hurricane‑force winds to test how they fail – and found clues for making affordable housing safer Published: October 9, 2026 8:06am EDT https://theconversation.com/we-blasted-mobile-homes-with-hurricane-force-winds-to-test-how-they-fail-and-found-clues-for-making-affordable-housing-safer-292074 https://theconversation.com/we-blasted-mobile-homes-with-hurricane-force-winds-to-test-how-they-fail-and-found-clues-for-making-affordable-housing-safer-292074 Link copied Share article Share article Copy link Email Bluesky Facebook WhatsApp Messenger LinkedIn X (Twitter) Print article It’s 9:15 in the morning. The wind is roaring at 110 miles per hour (177 kph); the sound is deafening. Dust swirls through the air. We are watching a single-wide manufactured home rock back and forth, as it is pounded by turbulent wind. Then, in an instant, the screws shear off and the entire unit tumbles backward, rolling two full revolutions before it stops. In any other situation, this would be terrifying. But here, we are conducting a [first-of-its-kind experiment](https://news.fiu.edu/2026/fius-wall-of-wind-tests-manufactured-home-across-various-u.s-installation-practices) at Florida International University’s [Wall of Wind](https://theconversation.com/30-years-after-hurricane-andrew-devastated-florida-researchers-are-using-a-wall-of-wind-to-design-safer-homes-but-storms-are-getting-even-more-intense-183510), a test facility that can reproduce wind speeds the equivalent of a Category 5 hurricane. We are testing a full-scale manufactured home to measure the effects of hurricane-force winds and identify strategies that can improve the safety of all mobile homes as disaster risk rises. We want disaster-resilient and affordable housing – not just one, but both. How a mobile home rolled in high winds during testing in FIU’s Wall of Wind. As [structural](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=PPvrXaEAAAAJ&hl=en) and [wind engineering](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=puvjTZUAAAAJ&hl=en) researchers, we have been working on this problem for a decade. Our [recent Wall of Wind experiment](https://news.fiu.edu/2026/fius-wall-of-wind-tests-manufactured-home-across-various-u.s-installation-practices) was the first time the federal design requirements for manufactured homes had ever been put to the test in a controlled environment with sensors and videography to capture the effects. The results of our work show that manufactured housing can be built safely. For that to happen, state and federal building codes need a serious update. Finding the weaknesses, and solutions To back up for a moment, one thing you should know is that the design and construction of manufactured homes are regulated by a [specific federal building code](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/subtitle-B/chapter-XX/part-3280) with wind engineering standards from 1988. The antiquated federal code lacks any guidance for building these homes to withstand earthquakes, wildfires or tornadoes. It also created differences in construction costs and performance between manufactured housing and site-built housing, which is regulated by a [different building code system](https://www.nist.gov/buildings-construction/understanding-building-codes) that is updated every three years. The cost differences are critical and what keeps manufactured homes affordable. The performance differences can be deadly. Over [22 million people live in manufactured homes](https://www.manufacturedhomes.com/blog/manufactured-housing-share-vs-site-built/) in the U.S., as they are the [most common type of unsubsidized affordable housing](https://www.urban.org/research/publication/building-climate-resilient-manufactured-housing-stock). Our research is investigating ways to enhance the performance of manufactured homes without compromising affordability. Hurricane Irma’s winds pushed mobile homes off their pad at the Enchanted Shores manufactured home park in Naples, Fla., in September 2017. [Nicholas Kamm/AFP via Getty Images](https://www.gettyimages.com/detail/news-photo/an-overturned-trailer-is-seen-at-the-enchanted-shores-news-photo/846035164?adppopup=true) By integrating structural engineering laboratory experiments, post-disaster field investigations and community engagement, we are finding new solutions to increase building strength, reduce loads and optimize the use of construction materials. When we started, there was a dearth of research on factory-built housing and no clear public documentation on how these structures are constructed. So, we began examining [how mobile and manufactured](http://doi.org/10.1061/(ASCE)CF.1943-5509.0001486) homes [failed during disasters](http://doi.org/10.1061/(ASCE)NH.1527-6996.0000476). Through testing, we observed failures in how the homes were [anchored to the ground](http://doi.org/10.1061/JPCFEV.CFENG-5309) and in how the [roof connects to the walls](http://doi.org/10.1061/(ASCE)CF.1943-5509.0001486). At the University of Kansas, we have now performed hundreds of experiments on [load path connections](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.engstruct.2026.123383) – measuring the stress on the connections between roof sheathing and rafters, roofs and walls, and walls and floors. We have also been testing new designs using thicker straps, screws and different hardware to enhance their strength. Changing a federal building code that has remained [stagnant for 32 years](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/subtitle-B/chapter-XX/part-3280) requires more than lab work. We also talk with residents, home and installation system manufacturers, installers, advocacy organizations, federal and state agencies and fellow researchers to learn from their perspectives what is and isn’t working. We have worked with the Federal Emergency Management Agency to update its approach to using mobile and manufactured homes as post-disaster temporary housing so that when disaster victims need housing, they can continue to receive it quickly, with assurance it was installed following best practices. And we’re translating our research into templates that will make it easier for state and local governments to adopt best practices for local installation requirements. Inside the Wall of Wind In spring 2026, we [tested three manufactured homes](https://news.fiu.edu/2026/fius-wall-of-wind-tests-manufactured-home-across-various-u.s-installation-practices) provided by FEMA. We examined seven installation systems commonly used in regions prone to hurricanes, floods or earthquakes. These tests are unique because the manufactured homes were installed by local experts in soil matching real-world conditions and tested at full scale against hurricane-force winds. A full-scale mobile home being tested at the Wall of Wind in June 2026. Margi Rentis/Florida International University We collected high-speed video imagery and tracked the movement of the manufactured home and stresses on its materials through time. The tests enabled us to document damage as it progressed, learning what fails first, second, and so forth that then ends with the unit rolling over. This damage progression is something that cannot be observed after the disaster or through testing small-scale models in wind tunnels. Our preliminary results reveal that installation practices allowed by some states do not meet the federal code minimum requirements, and that many commonly used installation systems in the U.S. do not meet modern wind engineering standards. There is good news too. The most robust installation systems performed effectively. Manufactured home residents can talk to their installers about meeting [Wind Zone III requirements](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/subtitle-B/chapter-XX/part-3285#subpart-E) – which may exceed the legal minimum requirement for where the home is going. Engineers Elaina Sutley and Arindam Chowdhury examine the anchoring system of a mobile home after a wind test. [Margi Rentis/Florida International University](https://news.fiu.edu/2026/fius-wall-of-wind-tests-manufactured-home-across-various-u.s-installation-practices) Our team also designed a wind load reduction device that attaches to the gutter system along the roof line. It reduces wind pressures on the roof, helping the roof shingles remain attached and intact under harsh wind conditions. And we collaborated with an industry team to test a new anchoring device that optimizes the use of the construction materials. The results demonstrate that manufactured homes can be designed and built to be both affordable and resilient to high winds. In fact, some already are; it just isn’t the norm. The mobile homes we tested were built specifically for FEMA at three times the strength required by the federal building code. When installed with a robust anchoring system, the homes we tested performed well even at high wind speeds. Safe, affordable housing in the future The state of housing in the U.S. is on an unsustainable path, with [rising costs](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/acs-1-year-estimates.html), inadequate construction quality and [frequent extreme weather events](https://theconversation.com/two-key-ingredients-cause-extreme-storms-with-destructive-flooding-why-these-downpours-are-happening-more-often-254123). Transforming the way manufactured homes are built and installed can save lives and reduce losses for millions of people, while making prefabricated housing a valuable solution to the affordable housing crisis. Our results show it can be done.
