The Fund said distortions arising from value-added tax (VAT) exemptions and the inability of businesses to recover taxes paid on production inputs can increase operating costs, with the additional burden cascading through supply chains and eventually reaching consumers. In its October 2026 Fiscal Monitor, launched at the ongoing IMF and World Bank Group Annual Meetings in Bangkok, Thailand, the Fund said governments could strengthen economic growth without sacrificing revenue by improving the design and implementation of their tax systems. The Fiscal Monitor was presented at a press briefing involving Rodrigo Valdés, director of the IMF’s Fiscal Affairs Department; Era Dabla-Norris, deputy director of the department; Davide Furceri, Division chief in the department; and Pavis Devahasadin, Communications officer at the IMF. The report noted that how governments raise revenue matters as much as how much they collect, warning that poorly designed taxes can discourage investment, distort business decisions and weaken the productive base needed to sustain future revenue generation. According to the IMF, VAT-related distortions can add between 2.3 percent and 8.9 percent to input costs across broad industry groups in the 26 economies studied, depending on the level of exposure to the tax distortions. The upper end of the range represents costs in the most exposed sectors and should not be interpreted as an economy-wide average. The Fund explained that businesses generally recover VAT paid on production inputs, but exemptions and non-registration can break the credit chain, turning the tax into an additional production cost. When a business cannot reclaim VAT paid on its inputs, the unrecovered amount increases its costs. These higher costs can then be passed to other businesses along the supply chain, ultimately contributing to higher prices for consumers. The report said eliminating the production and consumption distortions associated with existing VAT systems could generate annual welfare gains of up to 0.8 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) in the economies studied, with an average gain of 0.26 percent. The estimates assume that the reforms preserve overall revenue while allowing businesses to fully recover input VAT and applying the tax more uniformly to final consumption. The IMF argued that reducing such distortions would enable businesses to use resources more efficiently, lower production costs and improve competitiveness, including for exporters that can be disadvantaged when input taxes cannot be recovered. The report’s findings come amid mounting fiscal pressures confronting governments globally, with public debt historically high and projected to exceed 100 percent of global GDP before 2030. At the same time, real interest rates on government debt remain above pre-pandemic levels, while spending pressures and geopolitical tensions continue to complicate fiscal management. Global growth is also expected to remain subdued at about three percent over the medium term, making it more difficult for governments to strengthen public finances without undermining economic activity. The IMF said tax reforms could help address this challenge by reducing the economic costs associated with existing tax systems rather than relying exclusively on higher tax rates or additional revenue measures. Beyond VAT, the Fund identified corporate income taxation as another source of investment distortions, noting that typical corporate income taxes increase the cost of capital by an average of 14.7 percent in advanced economies and 19.2 percent in low-income developing countries. These taxes increase the pre-tax return that investment projects must generate to become commercially viable, potentially discouraging businesses from undertaking projects that would otherwise proceed. The report estimated that reforms allowing firms to deduct investment costs immediately could increase the long-term capital stock by 6.4 percent in advanced economies and 8.2 percent in low-income developing countries. Such reforms could also leave GDP between 2.1 percent and 2.7 percent higher, according to the Fund’s illustrative estimates. The IMF stressed, however, that improving the treatment of investment costs does not necessarily require governments to lower statutory corporate income tax rates. Instead, tax systems could be redesigned to reduce the burden on marginal investments while preserving the taxation of economic rents, or returns above what is required to make an investment viable. The Fund also highlighted the importance of tax administration, arguing that complex registration, filing, payment and refund procedures impose disproportionate costs on small and young businesses. Better tax administration can improve compliance, reduce uncertainty and free up resources that businesses could otherwise devote to production, investment and expansion. The report cited evidence associating stronger tax administration with tax revenue equivalent to about 1.7 percentage points more of GDP when comparing countries at the 67th and 33rd percentiles of an operational-strength index. It added that more efficient administration could help governments raise revenue without changing statutory tax rates, while reducing reliance on more distortionary tax instruments. The Fiscal Monitor’s analytical chapter also examined the effects of tax systems on employment, finding that revenue-neutral reforms to employment income taxation could raise employment rates by 1.2 percentage points in emerging market and developing economies and 0.8 percentage point in advanced economies, with the largest gains among lower earners. The report further cautioned that tax incentives favouring particular firms or business sizes can discourage expansion, encourage underreporting of sales and prevent businesses from reaching more productive scales. It recommended carefully targeting incentives towards activities with clear economic benefits, such as research and development, rather than assuming that preferential tax treatment automatically generates additional investment or innovation. The report’s central message is that governments can improve the contribution of taxation to economic growth by reducing distortions in tax design, strengthening administration and ensuring that businesses and workers retain appropriate incentives to invest, produce and participate in the labour market.
