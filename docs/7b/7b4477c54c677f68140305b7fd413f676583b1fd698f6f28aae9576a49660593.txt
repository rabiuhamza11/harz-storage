Given that genuinely undecided voters are scarce — [as little as 5%](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/16-undecided-voters-are-weighing-2026-election-options-rcna599899) to [7% of the electorate](https://thehill.com/homenews/campaign/6105250-voters-america-wrong-direction-midterms-poll/), according to major polls — both Democrats and Republicans must look to mobilize their rank-and-file supporters. To do so, the Republican Party has bet strongly this year on [a strategy of convincing people](https://revealnews.org/podcast/voter-fraud-christian-adams-elections-rights/) that [voter fraud is rampant](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/07/us/politics/trump-mail-voting-elections-fact-check.html). [As a scholar of U.S. politics](https://polisci.richmond.edu/faculty/cmiller3/), I see this as a high-risk strategy for Republican candidates and American democracy. That’s because reduced trust in election integrity can [reduce motivation to vote](https://statesunited.org/resources/when-americans-trust-elections/) – even if based on flawed information. But it’s not at all clear which Americans are most likely to stay home in November. Voter fraud vs. reality [Voter fraud is not a real problem](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/crsj/resources/human-rights/2026-september/debunking-voter-fraud-myth-again/) in the United States. Study after study, by [public officials](https://www.senatoredmcbroom.com/senate-oversight-committee-releases-report-on-november-2020-general-election/) and [think tanks](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/analysis-heritage-foundations-database-undermines-claims-recent-voter), has shown that [voting by noncitizens and other ineligible voters](https://www.cato.org/testimony/restoring-election-integrity-oversight-voter-fraud-new-jersey) happens only at minuscule, statistically insignificant levels. Nonetheless, [rhetoric from the Trump administration](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/americans-increased-belief-voter-fraud-180029721.html) about voter fraud has been pervasive. Consider the recent claim by Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin that [Nevada had nearly 16,000 noncitizens](https://www.dhs.gov/news/2026/07/17/dhs-secretary-markwayne-mullin-sends-letters-secretaries-state-warning-about-non) on its voter rolls – a number reduced to 185 when state authorities pressed for details, and [reduced to zero](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/24/us/politics/nevada-voters-homeland-security-trump.html) when the state investigated the names provided. There is comparable behavior from Congress. [My own research](https://doi.org/10.1080/27671127.2025.2478863) on political communication has shown that a substantial number of members of Congress, strongly skewing Republican, make frequent use of rhetoric that undermines democratic norms and delegitimizes political institutions. The strategy ties readily into President Donald Trump’s [persistent false claims that he won the 2020 presidential election](https://www.propublica.org/article/big-lie-trump-stolen-election-inside-creation). It also ties into his [administration’s strongly anti-immigrant agenda](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/02/americans-overwhelmingly-support-deporting-criminal-illegals-local-cooperation-with-ice/), which spreads fear of [noncitizens corrupting U.S. politics](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/1-in-3-fears-immigrants-influence-us-elections-ap-norc-poll). How does this uncertain political environment influence voters? There is [clear data showing](https://yankelovichcenter.ucsd.edu/public-engagement/CTTE-Report-2025-2026_2.17.pdf) that voter confidence in elections has fallen across party lines: Only 65% of Republicans, 64% of Democrats and 57% of independent voters are very or somewhat confident that the 2026 vote count will be reliable. [Conventional wisdom suggests](https://statesunited.org/resources/when-americans-trust-elections/) such discouraging numbers would depress turnout. It’s not as simple as that, however. A ‘no voter fraud’ sign is displayed in support of President Donald Trump at the Maricopa County Elections Department office on Nov. 4, 2020, in Phoenix. [Courtney Pedroza/Getty Images](https://www.gettyimages.com/detail/news-photo/no-voter-fraud-sign-is-displayed-by-a-protester-in-support-news-photo/1229466917?phrase=voter%20fraud&adppopup=true) What motivates voters Extensive research confirms the axiom that a sense of civic duty is the [strongest predictor of whether someone will vote](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-018-9459-3). It supersedes trust, partisanship and policy preferences. Any campaign’s quest to mobilize additional voters, therefore, must find them among citizens without a strong sense of civic duty. But among these people, diminished trust in democratic processes can play out in counterintuitive ways. [Recent research on German voters](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41269-026-00442-9) is relevant to the U.S.: It shows that among more passive voters – those following cues from political elites – reduced trust in elections can depress motivation to vote. That’s because there’s no trustworthy result that voters are seeking to affirm. Conversely, among voters with greater skepticism about elite messaging, reduced trust can motivate increased turnout as an attempted corrective for a flawed system. What does this mean for potential voters in the U.S.? Among Republicans, it appears that the party’s strategy carries a strong risk of backlash. [Findings from MIT’s Election Lab](https://electionlab.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/voter-trust.pdf) confirm that cues from political elites are a powerful factor in voter behavior. Specifically, cues setting expectations of a disappointing or untrustworthy result will reduce motivation. Why vote if you don’t expect it to help achieve the desired result? The same study finds Republican voters broadly inclined to believe GOP leaders’ cues about voter fraud and to frame their expectations accordingly. And once they’ve made that shift, they [cling firmly to the belief](https://electionlab.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/voter-trust.pdf), even in the face of contrary empirical evidence or messaging from within the party. In the current election cycle, 50% of [Republicans surveyed](https://yankelovichcenter.ucsd.edu/public-engagement/CTTE-Report-2025-2026_2.17.pdf) distrust mail-in voting, and 51% fear voting by noncitizens. They expect election results to be fraudulent. This likely won’t mobilize them, as there’s no positive result they can expect to affirm. The Republican Party’s messaging has painted its voters into a corner. Democrats, meanwhile, are less inclined to believe claims of voter fraud. But they are [more inclined to be concerned](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/10/us/politics/department-of-justice-election-fraud-states.html) about attempts to undermine the midterms by political elites making such claims. Examples include the Trump administration’s efforts to sow doubt about [voting by mail](https://theconversation.com/why-the-supreme-court-blocked-trump-and-the-postal-service-from-restricting-mail-in-voting-for-the-midterms-291319) and about alleged [noncitizen voting](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/25/nx-s1-5976804/supreme-court-trump-save-noncitizen-voting). These efforts increase motivation to vote among Democrats. Unlike GOP voters, they see the capacity to correct for the system’s vulnerabilities – the potential and urgency to overwhelm attempts at voter suppression through higher turnout. No matter the results this November, however, we’re left with a precarious dynamic. Increasing numbers of voters lack trust in elections and make their decisions to vote – or not – based on limited or flawed information. Fortunately, there are signs of hope. Although voters in general [are poorly informed](https://electionlab.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/voter-trust.pdf) about how elections are conducted, they can and do respond [to clear information](https://theconversation.com/election-officials-are-fighting-wavering-voter-trust-by-making-mundane-processes-visible-and-recruiting-citizens-to-help-292199) familiarizing them with election processes. I’ve seen this myself over the years, working with nonpartisan efforts to register young adults and train them as poll watchers. And in the current election cycle, there have already been [widespread efforts](https://protectdemocracy.org/work/frequently-asked-questions-about-the-2026-midterm-elections/) to [broaden public awareness](https://karshinstitute.virginia.edu/news/emergency-powers-and-elections-whats-stake-2026) about the fact that state officials, not federal ones, [have authority over voter rolls](https://statedemocracy.law.wisc.edu/our-work/can-the-federal-government-force-states-to-hand-over-citizens-voter-information) and election security. Meanwhile, [fewer Americans are expected to vote by mail this year](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/voters-trust-local-election-officials-theyre-less-sure-about-the-mail) – whether due to fear of fraud or [fear of the Post Office](https://www.lwv.org/newsroom/news-clips/postmaster-generals-remarks-mail-ballots-stoke-fears-among-voting-rights). If they instead vote in person, a silver lining is that positive [personal experiences with poll workers](https://doi.org/10.1177/1065912908324870) are also associated with increased voter trust. Looking beyond November, what Americans can hope for is a cycle in which clear election outcomes provide an opportunity to inform voters of how and why the system works. That’s the way to motivate more political engagement and voter turnout in the future.
