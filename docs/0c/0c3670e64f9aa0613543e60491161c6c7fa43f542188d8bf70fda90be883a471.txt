1.0 INTRODUCTION 
Since the last Fiscal Strategy Paper (2025 - 27), economic developments have been in a 
positive direction, and the federal government remains committed to maintaining policy mix as 
it looks ahead to consolidate and advance inclusive economic growth. 
 
The 2026 - 2028 Medium Term Expenditure Framework and Fiscal Strategy Paper 
(MTEF/FSP) is being developed at a time of cautious optimism for the Nigerian economy. It 
builds on recent gains made through bold structural reforms and policy recalibrations, while 
confronting persistent vulnerabilities and uncertainties in the global and domestic 
environment. The period ahead presents Nigeria with both an opportunity and a responsibility 
to sustain macroeconomic stability, deepen structural transformation, and entrench inclusive 
and resilient growth across all sectors of the economy. 
 
Over the past two years, the Federal Government has made significant strides in stabilizing 
the macroeconomic landscape. Real GDP growth improved to 3.4 percent in 2024, the 
strongest pace since 2014 (outside the COVID-19 recovery period) driven by continued 
recovery in the oil sector and robust growth in ICT and financial services. While inflation 
remained elevated, averaging above 25 percent in 2024, a tight monetary stance by the 
Central Bank of Nigeria and complementary fiscal discipline have begun to restore confidence 
in economic management. The unification of the foreign exchange market, adoption of a 
transparent FX trading platform, and sustained fiscal consolidation efforts have contributed to 
a stronger external position, a firmer naira, and growing investor interest. 
 
These reforms have not been without cost. The removal of fuel subsidies and other 
adjustments have placed short-term pressures on household welfare and business 
operations, but they are necessary and overdue steps to reposition the economy for long-term 
competitiveness. Following the enactment of the Nigerian Tax Acts with implementation 
expected to commence in 2026, improved revenue outturns, relative expenditure discipline, 
and a narrowing fiscal deficit are expected positive signals of the early impact of these reforms. 
 
The focus of the 2026 - 2028 MTEF/FSP is to consolidate these gains and accelerate progress 
toward a more diversified and resilient economy. The assumptions underpinning the 
framework (Chapter 5) reflect moderate global growth, a stable but still volatile oil market, and 
domestic reforms aimed at boosting productivity and investment. The medium-term 
macroeconomic framework (Chapter 6) is designed to balance growth aspirations with fiscal 
prudence, setting realistic targets for revenue mobilisation, expenditure efficiency, and deficit 
reduction. 
 
Key to the medium-term strategy is the government’s continued investment in physical and 
social infrastructure, including energy, transport, education, and health, as well as a renewed 
focus on human capital development and job creation. Enhanced public financial management 
systems, especially through digital transformation and automation, will underpin efforts to 
close leakages, improve tax compliance, and ensure that every naira spent delivers value for 
money. 
 
The Federal Government remains committed to maintaining debt sustainability. Chapter 8 
provides a comprehensive analysis of Nigeria’s debt stock, contingent liabilities, and the 
evolving debt management strategy. Debt accumulation will remain guided by cost-risk trade-
offs and targeted at high impact, growth enabling investments, consistent with Nigeria’s fiscal 
responsibility framework. 
 
The risks to Nigeria’s fiscal and economic outlook (Chapter 9) are not trivial. Geopolitical 
tensions, volatility in the international oil market, global inflation persistence, and domestic 

2 
 
security challenges continue to pose threats to stability. However, the government’s strategy 
is to anticipate, mitigate, and respond to these risks with sound macroeconomic management 
and flexible policy instruments. 
 
This MTEF/FSP thus represents more than a technical pre-budget statement. It is a 
consolidation of the government’s economic recovery efforts and a reaffirmation of its 
commitment to inclusive, job rich growth. It lays the foundation for the 2026 Appropriation Bill 
and signals to the Nigerian people, development partners, and investors that Nigeria remains 
on a steady path to economic transformation, backed by reform resolve and fiscal realism.

2.0 ECONOMIC AND FISCAL DEVELOPMENTS 
2.1 Global Economic Developments and Implications 
Global economic growth is projected to moderate as temporary trade-related support 
mechanisms fade. Growth is forecast at 3.0% in 2025 and 3.1% in 2026, down from 3.3% in 
2024. While these projections exceed the IMF April 2025 reference forecast, they remain 
below the pre-pandemic historical average of 3.7%. 
 
Advanced economies face significant headwinds, with growth projected at 1.5% in 2025 and 
1.6% in 2026. The euro area is expected to see modest acceleration from 1.0% in 2025 to 
1.2% in 2026, though these levels remain historically subdued. Regional performance varies 
considerably. Middle East and Central Asia show the strongest prospects, with growth 
accelerating from 3.4% in 2025 to 3.5% in 2026. Sub-Saharan Africa demonstrates resilience 
with stable growth at 4.0% in 2025 before strengthening to 4.3% in 2026. Latin America and 
the Caribbean face temporary deceleration to 2.2% in 2025 before recovering to 2.4% in 2026. 
Emerging and Developing Europe presents the most challenging outlook, with sluggish growth 
at 1.8% in 2025 improving modestly to 2.2% in 2026. 
 
The IMF's upward revision reflects strong front-loading in international trade, lower worldwide 
effective tariff rates than initially assumed, and improved global financial conditions. This front-
loading effect is expected to reverse in coming quarters, creating headwinds for 2026 activity. 
The United States economy is projected to expand 1.9% in 2025, supported by tariff rates 
settling below April 2025 announcements and accommodative financial conditions. Global 
headline inflation is expected to decline to 4.2% in 2025 and 3.6% in 2026, with cross-country 
variation. US inflation will likely remain above target while other major economies experience 
more subdued price pressures.

2.2 Domestic Economic Environment 
GDP growth and Sectoral Performance 
 
Nigeria's economy demonstrated robust performance with real GDP growth of 4.6% year-on-
year in Q4 2024, delivering full-year growth of 3.4%—the highest since 2014 excluding the 
COVID-19 rebound period. This acceleration was driven by continued oil and gas sector 
recovery and particularly strong services growth, notably in ICT and finance. Agricultural 
performance remained constrained at 1.2% growth due to elevated input costs and security 
challenges.

2.3 Unemployment and Underemployment 
Labour force participation declined to 77.3% in Q1 2024 from 79.5% in Q3 2023, with men 
and women showing similar participation rates of 77.5% and 77.1% respectively. Rural areas 
maintained higher participation rates than urban centers. 
 
Employment-to-population ratio stood at 73.2% in Q1 2024, with minimal gender disparity 
(72.4% for men, 72.3% for women). Rural areas significantly outperformed urban areas with 
employment ratios of 78.9% versus 69.5%. Self-employment remains dominant, accounting 
for 84% of employed Nigerians, with women showing higher self-employment rates (87.9%) 
than men (79.9%). Rural self-employment rates (91.9%) substantially exceed urban rates 
(78.2%). 
The unemployment rate increased to 5.3% 
in Q1 2024 from 5.0% in Q3 2023. Gender 
disparities persist, with female 
unemployment (6.2%) exceeding male 
unemployment (4.3%). Urban 
unemployment (6.0%) remains higher than 
rural unemployment (4.3%). Youth 
unemployment reached 8.4%, highlighting 
the need for targeted policy interventions. 
Underemployment affected 10.9% of 
employed Nigerians in Q1 2024, with youth 
underemployment (ages 15-24) at 14.4%, 
indicating significant labour market 
inefficiencies requiring policy attention.

2.5 Fiscal Sector 
Government Revenue 
Fiscal position has been 
constrained by persistently low 
government revenues, limiting the 
authorities' capacity to meet fiscal 
obligations and finance 
development priorities. In 
response, the government has 
undertaken comprehensive 
revenue mobilization reforms 
targeting both tax administration 
and oil sector challenges, 
including pipeline vandalism and 
crude oil theft. The tax-to-GDP 
ratio improved markedly to 10.9% 
from approximately 5-6% in 
previous years, driven primarily by 
non-oil revenue expansion and the incorporation of previously excluded federal and sub-
national revenue streams in official statistics. This adjustment provides a more accurate 
representation of Nigeria's revenue performance, though the ratio remains below regional 
benchmarks. 
The authorities are implementing a multi-pronged reform strategy focused on enhancing 
revenue collection efficiency. Key initiatives include process automation, strengthened fiscal 
prudence measures emphasizing value-for-money principles, and institutional reforms to 
optimize tax revenues and government enterprise remittances. Tax harmonization efforts aim 
to eliminate multiple taxation while improving voluntary compliance through streamlined 
procedures. 
These structural reforms are expected to yield further revenue gains as implementation 
deepens. However, sustaining these improvements will require continued political 
commitment to reform implementation and addressing underlying constraints in the oil sector 
that have historically undermined fiscal performance. 
Total revenues exhibited strong growth in 2024, increasing by 70% to N7.23 trillion from N4.25 
trillion in 2023. This improvement was broad-based, with both oil and non-oil revenues 
contributing to the expansion. Oil revenues rose by 81.8% to N2.0 trillion, constituting 27.7% 
of total revenues, driven by increased crude oil production averaging 1.49 million barrels per 
day (mbpd) compared to 1.41 mbpd in 2023. Non-oil revenues grew by 65.3% to N5.22 trillion, 
representing 72.3% of total collections and reflecting the impact of ongoing tax administration 
reforms. 
The revenue gains are expected to strengthen fiscal capacity and support implementation of 
the government's development agenda. However, despite improvements in non-oil revenue 
performance, Nigeria's tax-to-GDP ratio remains below comparator countries with similar 
economic fundamentals, indicating substantial scope for further revenue mobilization. 


9 
 
Tax system reforms are progressing, with implementation of the Presidential Tax Reform 
Committee's recommendations underway. International recognition of these efforts is 
emerging, as evidenced by Nigeria's inclusion in the 2024 Tax Transparency in Africa Report, 
which noted progress in developing the country's Exchange of Information framework to 
combat tax evasion through enhanced transparency mechanisms among participating 
jurisdictions. 
While the revenue trajectory is encouraging, sustaining these gains will require continued 
strengthening of tax administration capacity and addressing structural challenges in the oil 
sector. The authorities' commitment to comprehensive tax reform positions Nigeria to 
gradually close the revenue gap with regional peers. 
 
Government Expenditure 
Government expenditure remains a key driver of socioeconomic development. Government 
channels spending to areas of 
priority with a view to enhancing 
and promoting inclusive growth. 
The persistent growth in federal 
spending is attributable to the 
government’s investment in 
critical infrastructures and social 
protection schemes. 
Expansionary fiscal measures 
have been employed to spur 
economic activities by putting 
more money into the hands of 
consumers and businesses as a 
key part of government’s policy 
response to accelerate recovery in the wake of the last two successive economic recessions. 
 
Ensuring fiscal discipline and effective allocation of resources to the different sectors, as well 
as efficiency in MDAs service delivery, guarantees value for money for the government. FGN 
expenditure rose by 13% from N4.95 trillion in 2023 to N5.60 trillion in Q4 2024. Recurrent 
spending increased by 26% to N4.21 trillion in Q4 2024 and accounted for 75.13% of total 
outlay. Capital spending dropped by 25% to N0.96 trillion and accounted for 17.1% of the 
overall spending. 
To facilitate inclusive economic growth, the allocation of the capital outlay prioritizes projects 
in key infrastructure sectors like works and power, as well as in the area of human capital 
development, especially health and education. The government also invests appreciable 
amount of its outlays to offer succour to the poor to cushion the effects of the unintended 
negative but transient effects of reforms. Overall, the fiscal operations of the federal 
government resulted in a deficit of N3.08 trillion in 2024. The fiscal deficit was mainly financed 
through domestic borrowing. A direct result of the inadequate revenues and persistent fiscal 
deficit is the significant increase in public debt overtime.

Public Debt 
As of December 31, 2024, the total public debt was US$94.2 billion, of which US$45.7 billion 
or 48.5% was external debt and US$48.4 billion or 51.4% domestic debt. The total debt 
increased to US$97.2 billion at the end of the first quarter of 2025, with external debt at 
US$45.9 billion and domestic debt US$51.2 billion. The FGN’s share of the total public debt 
was US$86.8 billion or 92.16% in 2024 and US$48.7 billion or 50.13% by the first quarter of 
2025. 
Source: Debt Management Office 
Sustainable debt management is a key priority of the fiscal authorities in Nigeria. Borrowing 
activities, debt servicing, and debt sustainability are managed and monitored by the Debt 
Management Office (DMO) with the primary objective of ensuring that Nigeria's debt remains 
sustainable and does not pose a significant risk to the country's economy. 
 
The external debts are mainly due to borrowing from the multilateral, bilateral, commercial, 
and syndicated sources. Over the years, the DMO has proactively developed more debt 
instruments to enable the government further access the domestic debt market. This also 
offers different categories of retail, corpoarte and institutional investors, the opportinties to 
invest in the sovereign debt instruments. While the FGN Bonds and the Nigerian Treasury 
Bills are the dorminant domestic debt instruments, the Savings Bond, Green Bond, Sukuk, 
and the Promissiory Notes are also issued as part of the local debt instruments. 
While multilateral and bilateral loans are concessionary, the commercial and syndicated 
borrowings are on commercial terms. As at December 31, 2024, borrowings from the 
multilateral window was US$600 million or 55.7% of the total external loans, while US$1.08 
million or 4% was from bilateral sources. Borrowing on commercial terms through the 
Eurobond was US$148 million or 13.8%, while the syndicated loans totalled US$280 million 
or 26% of the total external debt for 2024. Given that almost half of the borrowing are from 
concessionary sources, it reduces the interest payment burden that would have been incurred 
from the commercial loans.

2.6 External Sector 
Trade 
Foreign trade stood at N36.6 trillion in Q4 2024, total exports stood at N20 trillion, and total 
imports amounted to N16.6 trillion. Total imports increased by 13% compared to the value 
recorded in the third quarter of 2024 (N14.67 trillion) but increased by 68.32% when compared 
to the value recorded in the corresponding quarter of 2023 (N21.75 trillion). However, by the 
end of Q1 2025, foreign trade stood at N36 trillion indicating a decline in trade activity. Total 
exports for the period amounted to N20.6 trillion while imports stood at N15.4 trillion. 
Source: National Bureau of Statistics

3.0 REVIEW OF 2024 BUDGET PERFORMANCE 
The performance of key budget parameters in the 2024 fiscal year indicates a mixed picture 
of gradual economic recovery amid persistent structural and macroeconomic challenges. 
While the average crude oil price exceeded the budget benchmark, providing some cushion 
to oil revenue projections, actual oil production volumes fell significantly short of the budgeted 
target due to ongoing issues, including pipeline vandalism, oil theft, and underinvestment in 
upstream infrastructure. 
Inflation remained above the projected levels, driven by exchange rate volatility, rising energy 
and food costs, and the continued effects of fuel subsidy removal. Real GDP growth also 
underperformed relative to expectations, constrained by weak performance in critical sectors 
and limited fiscal space to stimulate growth. 
Furthermore, the 2024 fiscal year was marked by substantial depreciation of the naira, mainly 
resulting from the transition to a more market-determined exchange rate regime, external 
imbalances, and heightened demand pressures in the foreign exchange market. These 
developments collectively posed significant risks to budget implementation, eroded 
purchasing power, and amplified the cost of servicing foreign-denominated obligations.

4.0 REVIEW OF 2025 BUDGET IMPLEMENTATION 
The FY 2025 Budget Implementation Review indicates measured progress in both revenue 
mobilization and expenditure management. While actual collections fell slightly short of 
prorated targets, the overall fiscal trajectory remains broadly on track. Notably, key non-oil 
revenue streams showed mixed performance, with some underperforming relative to 
projections, while others, such as CIT and VAT, exceeded expectations. 
On the expenditure side, releases have been cautiously managed in line with revenue outturns 
and the extended implementation of the FY 2024 Budget. This prudent approach reflects the 
government’s effort to balance fiscal discipline with the need to sustain essential spending 
commitments. 
Ongoing reforms in tax administration, expenditure control, and revenue diversification are 
gradually strengthening the government’s capacity to mobilize resources, thereby laying the 
groundwork for improved fiscal stability and medium-term growth. However, persistent oil 
production shortfalls continue to pose the single most significant risk to revenue performance, 
underscoring the urgency of accelerating both oil sector reforms and non-oil revenue 
expansion.

5.0 ASSUMPTIONS UNDERLYING 2026 - 2028 REVENUE PROJECTIONS 
5.1 Economic Growth 
The Nigerian economy is projected to grow in real terms by 4.7% in 2026, up from the 3.38% 
recorded post-rebasing in 2024 and 4.6% estimated for 2025. Through increased 
productivity and strategic investments, real GDP is expected to accelerate to 6% by 2027, 
reaching 7.9 in 2028 and over 9% by 2030. This growth trajectory aligns with the Renewed 
Hope Agenda’s eight Priority Areas, and positions Nigeria to achieve the $1 trillion economy 
target. The projected real GDP growth is expected to be driven by strong services, a rebound 
in agriculture, and improved industrial activity amid a more stable domestic environment, 
favourable global economic conditions and the present Administration's overall development 
policy thrust. In dollar terms, the country’s GDP is expected to increase from US$348.4 billion 
in 2023 to US$363.8 billion, projected for 2026. 
5.2 Inflation Rate 
Inflation is projected to decline remarkably in the medium term, from an average of 16.5% in 
2026 to 13.0% in 2027, and 9.0% in 2028 ±2.0. As of July 2025, inflation rate stood at 21.9% 
but averaged 23.2% from January to July. Key drivers supporting the price outlook include: 
• Foreign exchange stability, stemming from ongoing Central Bank reform measures. 
• Tight monetary stance, reflecting the Central Bank's firm commitment to price stability. 
• Enhanced fiscal-monetary policy coordination. 
• Improved fiscal spending allocation. 
• Easing supply pressures and increased agricultural production. 
• Falling transportation costs. 
• Transition to inflation-targeting framework and ongoing fiscal reforms. 
• Use of public borrowings for productive supply-side investments. 
• Election-Related Spending in 2026. 
5.3 Exchange Rate 
The exchange rate exerts significant influence on economic projections in Nigeria as it impacts 
oil revenues, non-oil foreign trade, and foreign currency expenditures. The exchange rate also 
affects foreign currency-denominated expenditures like Foreign Missions, Immigration 
attaché, Defence Missions, and counterpart funding for GAVI – the Vaccine Alliance. 
The CBN expects the Naira exchange rate to be generally stable over the medium term, 
oscillating from an average of N1,500 per US$ in 2025 to N1,512 per US$ in 2026, and 
appreciating to N1,432.15 per US$ in 2027. This expectation is based on more efficient foreign 
exchange market, appropriate monetary policy stance, and improved foreign exchange supply 
due to sustained rebound of economic activities, growing non-oil exports, stronger remittances 
inflow, and increased domiciliary account conversions. Current efforts towards effective oil 
sector management are expected to increase oil production and significantly contribute to the 
achievement of the medium-term target. Measures are in place to prevent speculative 
activity and the central bank has embedded best practices into the market’s 
operational framework, including foreign exchange code of conduct and revised foreign

5.4.2 Crude Oil Production and Demand 
OPEC expects global oil demand to increase by 4.8 mbpd over the medium-term 
period, rising from 105.0 mbpd in 2025 to 109.8 mbpd by 2028. This represents an 
average annual increase of 1.6 mbpd. This growth pattern is expected to be driven largely by 
strong and persistent crude oil demand growth in non-OECD countries. Key factors underlying 
the OECD demand include rising population growth, urbanization and continued robust 
economic growth. 
Despite current uncertainties surrounding the global economy and energy landscape, 
OPEC projects global oil supply to continually expand to meet rising oil demand. 
Market fundamentals are projected to remain supportive, as OPEC and its partners in 
the Declaration of Cooperation, and industry stakeholders work together to maintain market 
stability. 
In the medium term, global liquids supply is expected to increase by 11.2 mbpd from 102.4 
mbpd in 2024 to 113.6 mbpd in 2030. Global liquids supply growth in the medium term 
is expected to remain dominated by US liquids production, particularly by the ongoing 
rise in tight oil. Continued efficiency gains in production techniques, as well as 
sustained investment, are expected to result in continued liquids production. Several 
established producers, including Canada, Qatar and Norway, are also expected to 
contribute additional medium-term supplies alongside newer ‘frontier’ areas, including 
Brazilian ultra-deepwater and Argentinian tight oil. Additionally, barrels are anticipated

conducting independent value assessments to inform data-driven decisions, enforcing a 
robust performance management framework, and increasing transparency. 
5.5 Non-Oil Revenue Baseline Assumptions 
The assumptions underlying the non-oil revenue forecasts for the period 2026-2028 are 
outlined below. In view of the low revenue from crude oil, Government will implement various 
reform measures to further broaden the revenue base, modernize and further improve tax 
administration, and enhance non-oil revenue collections. Efforts will be geared towards 
ensuring better compliance and closing loopholes. This involves bringing more businesses 
and individuals into the tax net, particularly in the informal sector, which has historically been 
under-taxed. 
The medium-term non-oil revenue forecasts are based on effective implementation of these 
reform efforts in order to enhance the contribution of non-oil revenue sources to funding the 
FGN budget. The medium-term non-oil revenue estimates were estimated based on 
anticipated growth in the different tax bases, the effective tax rate, and the projected tax 
collection efficiency. Tax rates are assumed to remain the same in 2026. Tax revenue 
projections are based on forecast crude oil production, benchmark crude oil price, and 
exchange rate. Technical costs (T1 and T2) are based on 3-year average (2022-2024). 
Historical tax revenue collection for three years was used to arrive at the forecast values for 
the period. 
Customs Collections: Import Duties, Excise, Fees and Special Levies 
Import duty projections are based on the cost, insurance and freight (CIF) value of imports, 
applicable tariffs, and a projected efficiency factor. The growth of the nominal tax base is 

26 
 
assumed to be driven by tax elasticity in the medium term. Other considerations include the 
foreign exchange rate, effective implementation of extant tax laws, and the implementation of 
the Common External Tariff (CET) 2022-2026. The exchange rate is expected to vary 
minimally in 2026 within the average of the current rate in 2025. 
Revenue collections by the Nigeria Customs Service is expected to be driven by improved 
efficiency, enhanced port operations, reduction of manual/paper‐based processing, and 
improvements in cargo clearance times. Recent reforms include speedier inspection, the 
introduction of fast-track lanes for compliant importers, and streamlining processes in major 
ports like Apapa and Tin Can. 
More importantly, the effective implementation of the National Single Window project and 
large-scale digital overhaul of customs processing, surveillance, and payments under the E-
Customs Modernisation Project are expected to significantly facilitate transparent trade, and 
enhance customs revenue and efficiency. In addition, anti-smuggling efforts are being 
intensified, recovery from undervalued or under-assessed imports has increased and revenue 
leakages are being identified and acted upon. 
Companies Income Tax (CIT) 
The CIT projections are based on estimated nominal GDP, Companies’ Profitability Ratio, and 
further improvement in collection efficiency. The Gross Operating Surplus of firms used for 
CIT forecast is projected to average ₦38.92 trillion in 2026, ₦45.77 trillion in 2027, and ₦51.26 
trillion in 2028, after adjustments for informal sector firms and enterprises. Estimates were 
derived taking into consideration significant growth of domestic economic activities as well as 
the consolidation of ongoing reforms and effective implementation of the Renewed Hope 
Development Plan 2026-2030. Other important assumptions include significant improvement 
in the Nigerian business and investment environment, expected formalisation of businesses 
arising from the ongoing tax reforms and successful broadening of the tax net. 
Under the Nigerian Tax Act (NTA) 2025, Education Tax, Nigerian Police Trust Fund Levy, 
National Agency for Science and Engineering Infrastructure Levy and the National Information 
Technology Development Fund Levy have been replaced with Development Levy charged at 
4% of the company’s assessable profit. Similarly, Capital Gains Tax has been discontinued, 
with gains from the disposal of chargeable assets now subsumed under CIT. 
Value Added Tax (VAT) 
The Value Added Tax collection in the medium term was projected using estimated aggregate 
nominal consumption, taking into account vatable items and collection efficiency. VATable 
consumption expenditure is projected to increase from an average average of ₦446,309.37 
trillion in 2026, ₦525,936.08 trillion in 2027, and ₦642,245.15 trillion in 2028, after accounting 
for exemptions, zero-rated items, and companies below the ₦50 million turnover threshold. 
The Nigerian Tax (2025) allows taxpayers to claim input VAT on services and fixed assets, 
previously disallowed. This will likely increase VAT refund claims and slow the growth of Non-
import VAT revenue from 2026 onwards. Like the CIT, more VAT payers are expected to be 
brought into the tax net with the effective implementation of reform measures. The increase in 
firm turnover threshold from N25 million to N50 million and the reduced FGN share from 15% 
to 10% may further reduce the growth of VAT collections. 
The VAT projections over the medium-term are based on holding the rate at 7.5%. Raising 
the VAT rate however remains a medium-term policy option. In the medium term, Government 
will further intensify efforts aimed at improving VAT coverage and collection efficiency. Wider 

27 
 
coverage and improved collection efficiency will be achieved through nationwide VAT 
registration and monitoring, and deployment of ICT (auto-collect) platforms in more sectors of 
the economy. 
Tax refund projections were made based on the number and amount of refund applications 
received and processed in the last 2 years, macroeconomic factors including exchange rate 
and inflation, taxpayers who are perpetually in refund position by virtue of legislations and 
increased taxpayers’ awareness with regards to tax refunds 
Stamp Duty 
Under the Nigeria Tax Act (2025), the Electronic Money Transfer Levy (EMTL) has been 
renamed Stamp Duty, with liability shifting from the receiver to the sender. This change, along 
with the removal of the 1% duty on government contracts, may reduce overall stamp duty 
collections going forward. Compliance with the approved Regulations governing the 
administration of Stamp Duties will be enforced to ensure full collections over the medium 
term. Stamp duty is estimated based on the duties payable on chargeable instruments 
including tax stamps, electronic tagging, electronic receipts, and certificate issuance. 
FGN Independent Revenue 
The Independent revenue of the Federal Government was estimated taking into consideration 
recent efforts aimed at addressing revenue leakages, excessive spending and weak 
accountability of Government-Owned Enterprises (GOEs). The estimation of Operating 
Surpluses (the main component of FGN Independent Revenues) is based on strict and 
effective implementation of the various measures introduced to ensure that GOEs operate in 
a more fiscally responsible manner. Additional measures and tighter performance 
management framework will be introduced to ensure greater fiscal discipline among the GOEs 
and substantially improve remittances in the short to medium term. Accordingly, independent 
revenue collections are expected to be considerably higher than projections in the medium 
term.

6.2 2025-2027 Medium-Term Fiscal Framework (MTFF) 
6.2.1 Federation Account Revenues 
In the medium term, Federation Account revenues are projected to moderate, reflecting 
adjustments to key assumptions in the oil sector. Both crude oil production and the benchmark 
oil price have been revised downward to align with prevailing sector realities and the ongoing 
volatility in global energy markets driven by geopolitical tensions. While non-oil tax revenues 
are expected to record steady growth, these gains will be insufficient to offset the anticipated 
shortfall in oil-related inflows fully. 
For the 2026 fiscal year, total net accruals to the Federation Account are projected at ₦50.74 
trillion. Of this amount, ₦41.06 trillion is expected to flow into the Federation Account Main 
Pool, while ₦9.23 trillion and ₦456.07 billion are projected to accrue to the VAT Pool and 
Stamp Duty Pool, respectively. 
Oil revenues are estimated at ₦25.24 trillion, accounting for about 49.7% of total Federation 
Account receipts and 61.5% of Main Pool inflows. Other revenue components in the Main Pool 
include Corporate Tax (₦10.49 trillion), Customs Revenues (₦4.30 trillion), Special Levies 
(₦847.03 billion), NLNG Dividend (₦114.31 billion), and Solid Minerals (₦74.36 billion). 
From the Federation Account Main Pool, the Federal Government is projected to receive 
₦21.63 trillion, while the State and Local Governments are expected to receive ₦10.97 trillion

Stamp Duty collections, the Federal Government is expected to receive ₦45.61 billion, while 
the States and Local Governments are projected to receive ₦250.84 billion and ₦159.62

6.2.1 FGN Revenue 
The total revenue of the Federal Government of Nigeria (FGN) for the 2026 fiscal year is 
projected at ₦34.33 trillion, representing a decrease of ₦6.55 trillion or 16.0% over the 2025 
Budget estimate. This projection reflects a deliberate balance between realistic oil sector 
expectations and strengthened non-oil revenue mobilization efforts. 
Of the total projected revenue, ₦12.24 trillion (35.6%) is expected to be derived from oil-related 
sources, while ₦22.09 trillion (64.4%) will be generated from non-oil sources, underscoring 
the government’s ongoing efforts to diversify its fiscal base and reduce dependence on oil 
revenues. 
The FGN’s share of non-oil tax revenues is projected at ₦8.87 trillion in 2026, compared to 

31 
 
₦8.45 trillion in 2025, reflecting continued improvements in tax administration and compliance. 
Revenue from minerals and mining is expected to rise modestly to ₦36.07 billion, up from 
₦15.49 billion projected for 2025, driven by reforms in the solid minerals sector aimed at 
formalizing artisanal operations and improving royalty collections. 
Independent revenues from government-owned enterprises and agencies are projected at 
₦4.31 trillion, compared to ₦5.26 trillion in 2025. Although this reflects a moderation from the 
2025 projection, enhanced remittance enforcement and stronger performance monitoring 
frameworks are expected to deliver a more robust outcome. In addition, Grants and Donor-
funded Projects are expected to contribute ₦1.48 trillion (including $532.68 million consistent 
with the proposed US Government financial assistance in the health sector). 
The 2026 projections also recognize anticipated dividends from government equity holdings 
in institutions such as the Bank of Industry (BOI), Development Bank of Nigeria (DBN), Galaxy 
Backbone, and Nigeria LNG Limited (NLNG), estimated at ₦62.37 billion. This represents a 
significant reduction of about 90.9% relative to the 2025 estimate, reflecting more conservative 
assumptions and the evolving profitability outlook of these entities. 
Other revenues, including the FGN’s share of Oil Price Royalty, Development Levy, 
Drawdowns from Special Accounts, and Recoveries, are projected at ₦2.29 trillion in 2026. 
Overall, the 2026 revenue framework demonstrates the FGN’s commitment to fiscal 
consolidation, enhanced non-oil revenue generation, and strategic realignment of fiscal 
assumptions in line with emerging macroeconomic conditions and reform priorities.

6.2.3 Aggregate Expenditure 
The FGN has proposed an aggregate expenditure of ₦54.46 trillion for the 2026 fiscal year. 
This figure includes the projected spending of Government-Owned Enterprises (GOEs) 
amounting to ₦4.98 trillion, and ₦1.48 trillion for grants and donor-funded projects. The 

32 
 
proposed aggregate expenditure represents a decrease of ₦531.20 trillion, or 1.0%, less than 
the ₦54.99 trillion estimated in the 2025 Budget. 
The 2026 expenditure framework provides for statutory transfers of ₦3.15 trillion, debt service 
of ₦15.91 trillion (including ₦388.54 billion earmarked for the Sinking Fund to retire maturing 
bonds issued to local contractors and creditors), and non-debt recurrent expenditure of ₦15.27 
trillion. 
Personnel and pension costs are projected at ₦10.76 trillion, which includes ₦1.02 trillion for 
GOEs. This represents an increase of ₦768.55 billion, or 8.9%, compared to the 2025 
provision, reflecting adjustments for wage reviews, new recruitments in critical sectors, and 
pension obligations. 
In line with the government’s commitment to strengthening health services, ₦237.03 billion 
has been allocated as a first-line statutory transfer to the Basic Health Care Provision Fund 
(BHCPF) to improve access to quality primary healthcare services. In addition, ₦250.76 billion 
has been set aside under the Service-Wide Votes to support GAVI/Routine Immunisation and 
the Malaria Vaccination of Infants initiative. 
The aggregate capital expenditure in the 2026 Budget is projected at ₦22.37 trillion, 
representing 41% of total expenditure. This is, however, 14.6% lower than the capital 
allocation in the 2025 Budget, reflecting a more conservative approach to capital planning and 
the focus on ongoing projects. The capital budget components include: ₦8.68 trillion for MDAs, 
reflecting the rollover of about 70% of the 2025 capital budget; ₦3.15 trillion for capital 
supplementation; ₦2.24 trillion for the capital component of statutory transfers; ₦14 billion for 
the Family Home Fund; ₦2.96 trillion for capital projects of GOEs; ₦1.48 trillion for donor and 
grant-funded expenditures; ₦1.80 trillion as the capital component of the Development Levy; 
and ₦2.05 trillion for project-tied multilateral and bilateral loans. 
Overall, the 2026 expenditure framework underscores the FGN’s continued focus on fiscal 
consolidation, human capital development, and strategic capital investments to accelerate 
inclusive growth while maintaining fiscal sustainability.

6.2.4 Fiscal Deficit and Deficit Financing 
The fiscal deficit for the 2026 financial year is projected at ₦20.12 trillion, compared to ₦14.10 
trillion in 2025. This represents approximately 59% of total projected FGN revenues and 
3.61% of the estimated Gross Domestic Product (GDP). 
The widening deficit primarily reflects increased wage pressures, rising pension obligations, 
and higher debt service costs, following adjustments to the public sector remuneration 
framework and the impact of elevated domestic interest rates. These factors underscore the 
fiscal challenges of balancing expenditure priorities with the need for prudent debt 
management in an evolving macroeconomic environment. 
The 2026 fiscal deficit will be financed mainly through domestic borrowing, given the 
constrained access to external financing amid global interest rate tightening, heightened 
geopolitical risks, and cautious investor sentiment in emerging markets. Nonetheless, the 
financing mix will continue to emphasize debt sustainability, with a deliberate focus on 
optimizing the maturity profile of public debt, deepening the domestic debt market, and 
minimizing refinancing risks. 
Over the medium term, government strategy is geared towards a gradual fiscal consolidation 
path, anchored on enhancing non-oil revenue performance, rationalizing recurrent 
expenditure, and improving capital project efficiency. Key measures include broadening the 
tax base through ongoing tax policy and administration reforms, strengthening the 
performance and remittance compliance of Government-Owned Enterprises (GOEs), and 
promoting public-private partnerships (PPPs) to leverage private capital for infrastructure 
delivery. 
Collectively, these reforms are expected to reduce the fiscal deficit progressively over the 
medium term, create greater fiscal space for priority investments, and ensure long-term debt 
sustainability.

7.0 MEDIUM TERM OBJECTIVES, POLICIES AND STRATEGIES 
This Fiscal Strategy Paper highlights the macroeconomic and fiscal policy objectives of the 
government over the period 2026-2028 and the strategies to be implemented to achieve them. 
It was prepared against the backdrop of low economic growth, declining inflation, rising 
investment, improving revenues performance, infrastructure gaps and weak social indicators 
in the country. 
7.1 Macroeconomic Policy Objectives 
The outlook is very optimistic, supported by steady growth, easing inflation, political and fiscal 
stability, and a strong external position. However, the economy is vulnerable to oil price and 
climate shocks. In the medium term, government aims at consolidating macroeconomic 
stability, improving the business and investment environment, and achieving high, sustained 
and inclusive economic growth. Other key macroeconomic objectives include accelerating job 
creation and harnessing demographic dividends and improving human capital development 
and strengthening social protection. To achieve these objectives, fiscal, monetary and other 
economic policies will be sequenced, aligned and implemented in a very coordinated manner. 
7.1.1 Consolidating Macroeconomic Stability 
A stable macroeconomic environment is important to catalyse private investment and 
accelerate economic growth. It enables businesses and households to effectively plan their 
production, investment and consumption activities. Over the last two years, bold and 
comprehensive reforms implemented have removed distortions and laid a strong foundation 
for macroeconomic stability, resilience and positive economic outcomes. The output 
expansion, stabilized naira, falling inflation, rebuilt external buffers and rebalanced external 
accounts all signal a more stable macroeconomic environment and resilience to shocks. The 
authorities remain committed over the medium term to prudent policymaking and the durability 
of the reforms, ensuring a lasting and positive impact on the economy and growing prosperity. 
Government will further strengthen macroeconomic management and implement policies 
aimed at promoting higher investment and sustained inclusive growth. This includes strategies 
to moderate inflationary pressures, provide critical infrastructure to lower the cost of doing 
business, lower market interest rates, and sustain market reflective exchange rate through 
pragmatic foreign exchange management. Nigeria has transitioned to a transparent, market-
driven foreign exchange management system. It is projected that the Naira will stabilize and 
appreciate as macro-fiscal, monetary and trade policies become better aligned. The 
macroeconomic environment will be further stabilized as Nigeria implements the fiscally 
sustainable path of accelerated domestic revenue mobilisation and better expenditure 
management, moderate fiscal deficits, and a healthy debt sustainability framework. 
7.1.2 Improving Business and Investment Environment 
There is considerable improvement in consumer and investor confidence in the Nigerian 
economy in response to current efforts aimed at improving the business and investment 
environment for private investment to thrive and drive growth. Reduced distortions improve 
business climate and growth prospects and greater policy transparency from fiscal and 
monetary authorities raises confidence. In the medium term, Government is committed to 
addressing subsisting challenges impeding business growth. Additional business and 
investment-friendly reform measures will be introduced for businesses to thrive nationwide. 

36 
 
To foster healthy competition and investment, a level playing field will be provided among 
firms. The goal is to attract private sector led investment by maintaining strong business 
climate. 
Government will pursue necessary reforms in the regulatory environment to position Nigeria 
as an investment destination of choice. The leadership has demonstrated strong commitment 
and political will for the implementation of bold and progressive policies required to transform 
the economy. Government is also committed to ensuring robust and wide consultation with 
stakeholders in the medium term, within the framework of predictable, coherent and consistent 
policies. 
The Investment and Securities Act 2025 was enacted to improve regulatory oversight, market 
integrity and transparency to attract investment. The Act strengthens investor protection with 
stricter regulations and penalties for fraudulent activities, to bolster market confidence, and 
align Nigeria’s securities laws with global standards. In addition, the new tax Acts simplify the 
tax system, increasing compliance, and improving transparency to support investment. The 
fiscal incentives introduced in the oil and gas sector also aims to attract investments. 
Power infrastructure underpins economic growth, job creation, productivity, competitiveness, 
logistics, manufacturing and trade. In the medium term, government will further promote the 
ideals of a viable, competitive and inclusive electricity market by opening the sector to 
competition, provide affordable electricity to consumers and increase power 
generation/distribution capacity. Adjustments to cost reflective electricity tariffs will be phased, 
and targeted electricity subsidy will be implemented to protect vulnerable households. 
Government will also increase investment in capacity expansion of critical power grid 
infrastructure under the Presidential Power Initiative, especially projects that can be delivered 
quickly to optimize grid reliability, interconnectedness and wheeling capacity. 
Supported by the World Bank and the AfDB, a total of $32 billion has been mobilized under 
the Mission 300 initiative to ensure energy access and security and lower the cost of doing 
business. The Federal Government is also mobilising about $2 billion through key facilities 
such as the World Bank DARES programme, the NSIA RIPLE platform and the JICA fund, to 
drive renewable energy expansion and increase power access across rural and underserved 
communities. 
The sum of $2 billion has also been secured for the implementation of the Building Resilient 
Digital Infrastructure for Growth initiative (Project BRIDGE), a major digital-infrastructure PPP 
project. The main goal is to expand fibre optic coverage by 90,000 km, building on the existing 
35,000 km network with support from the World Bank and the AfDB. It is expected that these 
efforts will incentivize private investment thereby contributing towards realising the projected 
growth. Government will also enhance the access of small and medium enterprises in key 
sectors of the economy to sustainable finance to enhance their activities and improve their 
operational efficiency. 
The security situation has improved nationwide with recent Government defence and security 
investments. Expenditures related to national defence, border security, intelligence 
operations, internal security, counter-terrorism measures, and other security initiatives will 
continue to be accorded top priority in the medium term. The internal security architecture will 
be further overhauled, and new operational strategies will be introduced to enhance law 
enforcement capabilities and safeguard lives, properties and investments across the country. 

37 
 
The various services will work proactively and collaboratively to address subsisting security 
challenges nationwide to achieve enduring peace, stability and public safety in order to provide 
the secure environment required for economic activities to thrive in various communities. The 
improvement in the security situation will further improve the business and investment 
environment. 
7.1.3 Achieving High, Sustained and Inclusive Economic Growth 
The Nigerian economy is now more resilient to shocks and growth is expected to be broad-
based over the medium term, led by Agriculture, Industries, and Services sectors, all 
benefiting from the improved economic landscape. To realize the projected real GDP growth 
target and the trillion-dollar GDP in the medium term, high-impact initiatives will be 
implemented. The most productive and growth-enhancing sectors will continue to be 
prioritised in the allocation of resources, especially sectors that have job creation capacity and 
exports-orientation. The 2026 Budget will also integrate sustainability measures and green 
initiatives into budget planning to address climate change concerns, promote renewable 
energy sources, and reduce carbon emissions. 
Achieving high, sustained, and inclusive growth in the medium term requires a coordinated 
strategy focused on increasing productivity and diversifying the economic base. This involves 
deepening infrastructure investment to lower production costs and improve competitiveness, 
while promoting value-chain development in agriculture, manufacturing, and services to 
expand employment and exports. Fostering innovation and digitalisation will further stimulate 
private-sector-led growth and job creation. 
At the same time, growth must be inclusive and equitable, ensuring that its benefits are broadly 
shared across regions and social groups. Human-capital investments in education, 
healthcare, and skills development will enable greater participation in the economy, while 
social-protection programmes should safeguard vulnerable groups from shocks. By combining 
productivity-driven growth with inclusivity and institutional reform, a more resilient, broad-
based, and sustainable development trajectory will be achieved over the medium term. 
The Federal Government regards business owners/investors, whether local and foreign, as 
critical engines in the effort to achieve high, sustained and inclusive GDP growth. Government 
will design and implement measures to formalize the informal sector businesses and enhance 
their capacity to produce. In addition to improving their operational efficiency, enhancing the 
access of small and medium enterprises to sustainable financing would further accelerate 
economic growth. 
The establishment of the Renewed Hope Infrastructure Development Fund is a key driver for 
unlocking the country's economic potential, attracting investments, and positioning Nigeria as 
a competitive player in the global economy. Significant resources have been allocated to the 
Fund for critical infrastructure projects across the country, including roads, bridges, airports,

7.2 Fiscal Policy Objectives and Strategies 
Four inter-related laws were recently introduced to enhance Nigeria’s fiscal policy environment 
with effect from 1 January 2026. The Nigeria Tax Act 2025 consolidates and modernizes major 
existing tax laws into a unified national tax code, replacing many legacy statutes. The Nigeria 
Tax Administration Act 2025 establishes a single, coherent set of procedures to administer 
federally collected taxes, ensuring consistency and improving compliance. 
The Nigeria Revenue Service (Establishment) Act 2025 replaces the previous Service with a 
federal tax authority responsible for tax and non-tax revenue collection and enforcement. The 
Service was created to centralize and professionalize federal tax administration, improve 
compliance, reduce leakages, and create uniform processes for taxpayers regarding 
registrations, audits, and appeals. 
The Joint Revenue Board (Establishment) Act 2025 creates a statutory mechanism for 
revenue sharing and coordination between federal and sub-national revenue authorities. This 
aims to eliminate multiple taxation, reduce disputes over tax jurisdiction and improve sub-
national revenue mobilization. These Acts are intended to simplify law, reduce overlapping 
statutes, strengthen administration and compliance, and clarify federal-state revenue roles. 
In the medium term, Government aims to promote a coherent, equitable and efficient fiscal 
system that supports productive activities, delivers sustainable fiscal outcomes, and inclusive 
and competitive to achieve shared prosperity for the citizenry. Fiscal policy will be designed 
to empower and provide social support for the poor and vulnerable people. Government is 
committed to implementing transformative fiscal policy reforms aimed at achieving the

7.2.1 Enhancing Government Revenue 
Oil and non-oil revenue collections increased significantly recently driven by improved tax

7.3 Monetary Policy Objectives, Strategy and Forecasts 
7.3.1: Monetary Policy Objectives and Strategy 
The Central Bank of Nigeria continues to implement a medium-term monetary policy 
framework (2025 – 2027) to reduce the risk of time inconsistencies in policymaking and 
minimise overreaction due to temporary shocks. Consequently, benchmarks for monetary 
aggregates are generated using a Monetary Programme adapted from the IMF Financial 
Programming framework. The Programme takes into consideration the current 
macroeconomic fundamentals to achieve the Bank’s mandate of maintaining monetary, price, 
and financial system stability as a catalyst for inclusive growth. To better address persistent 
inflationary pressures and strengthen macroeconomic stability, the Bank is currently on the 
path of transition to the Inflation Targeting (IT) framework. To complement the new inflation-
targeting environment, the CBN will continue to monitor the growth in monetary aggregates to 
ensure that it remains consistent with the Bank's inflation objective. 
 
This report introduces annual inflation targets into the CBN benchmark in line with the inflation 
targeting implementation timelines. Like the experiences of most IT central banks, transitional 
inflation targets are set to enhance commitment and accountability from both the government 
and the central bank towards the achievement of low and stable inflation. The set targets were 
generated from the monetary programming scenario analysis. 

48 
 
 
In terms of implementation of monetary policy, the major goal of the Programme remains the 
minimisation of the adverse impact of market liberalisation, to ensure that the resulting 
inflationary buildup, following the policy implementation, is temporary. This policy is therefore 
intended to steer the economy towards achieving a low and stable inflation towards providing 
a conducive macroeconomic environment for sustainable growth. 
 
Furthermore, the conduct of monetary policy would align with the goals of the Medium-Term 
Expenditure Framework 2025-2030. In developing the Programme, the Bank carefully 
ensured that the projected monetary aggregates would support the Federal Government’s 
broader macroeconomic objectives. Though the economic fundamentals are bound to change 
while implementing these policies, the Bank, in collaboration with the fiscal authority, would 
periodically review the Programme and incorporate new developments in the macroeconomic 
landscape to ensure that monetary and fiscal policies follow a set path to reduce fiscal 
surprises that could adversely affect the actualisation of the programme outcomes. 
 
7.3.2 Monetary Policy Stance 
The Bank’s Monetary policy will primarily be aimed at ensuring macroeconomic and financial 
system stability, while the developmental roles would ensure that the Bank continues to 
support the overall macroeconomic objectives of the Federal Government, including the eight-
point agenda of the administration - food security, poverty eradication, economic growth and 
job creation, access to capital, inclusivity, improving security, rule of law and fighting 
corruption. In addition, the monetary policy of the Bank will support the long-term plan of 
the one trillion-dollar economy project of the current administration, which has already kick-
started in 2024. 
 
Specifically, the Programme aims at moderating the subsisting high inflation and creating an 
enabling environment for growth. Monetary policy will be flexible to rein in adverse trends in 
inflation in the programme period; hence, the Bank will continue to manage the growth in 
money supply within a non-inflationary path. Thus, the Monetary Policy Rate (MPR) and the 
Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and other instruments would be wielded appropriately in the 
programme period. These measures will complement the unification of the exchange rate 
windows and support broader policies aimed at achieving medium-term stability in the naira 
exchange rate. 
 
The Monetary Programme projections consider the various items in the 2025 FGN 
Appropriation Act and are built on more realistic projections of key economic indicators like 
Gross Domestic Product (GDP), Current and Capital Account Balances, Oil Production, Fiscal 
Deficit, etcetera. Specifically, the projections are predicated on the likely behaviour of key 
macroeconomic indicators in the short to medium term, and inflationary pressures could 
persist due to structural rigidities in the economy. Furthermore, a tighter monetary policy 
stance and supportive fiscal-monetary policy coordination, resulting in lower inflation and 
slower economic growth, is envisaged. 
 
In summary, the Bank will continue to focus on monetary, price, and financial system stability 
that delivers inclusive and sustainable growth in the period 2025 - 2030. Therefore, the Bank 
would maintain a contractionary monetary policy stance to subdue the current inflationary 
pressures. This would be achieved by the continuous deployment of prudential and liquidity 

49 
 
management tools to deliver the medium-term growth plan. The Bank would maintain a 
proactive stance to ensure the delivery of a sound, stable, and efficient financial system in the 
conduct of monetary policy. Meanwhile, recent developments in the general price level and 
exchange rate management necessitated the Bank's transition to an IT framework. Currently, 
broad money growth remains the intermediate target, while movements in the short-term 
interest rate would be closely monitored to counteract volatility and appropriately anchor 
market expectations. 
 
The current Programme projections are designed to accommodate the tightening stance of 
the Bank in the short to medium term, with the view to providing appropriate growth in 
monetary aggregates to achieve price stability in the economy. Additionally, the Programme 
is anchored on the assumptions of market liberalization and the private sector as a catalyst 
for economic growth.

7.3.4.1 Assumptions and Method for Setting Inflation Targets 
Setting the inflation targets under the monetary programming framework, we considered a mix 
of theoretical and empirical issues. The following were the considerations: 
▪ Quantity Theory of Money (QTM) was adopted – where inflation is expected to be equal 
to the difference between growth in money and real output, under the assumption of 
constant velocity and stable money multiplier. 
▪ The historical pattern or behaviour of inflation since the year 2003: 
▪ The pre- and post-election dynamics were analysed in adjusting the inflation 
outcomes 
▪ The averages of selected years reflecting the change in actual inflation were used 
to determine an adjustment factor – 0.87 per cent; 
▪ This factor was applied in adjusting the baseline inflation forecast for the period 2025 – 
2030. 
▪ The baseline forecast for 2028 was further discounted (0.70 per cent) to arrive at a target 
of 9.70 as a deliberate decision to achieve a single-digit inflation in the period. 
▪ The trend in global inflation was also considered in the determination of the final inflation 
targets.

7.3.5 Challenges to Monetary Policy and Domestic Economic Outlook

other unanticipated global shocks could further induce inflationary pressures with negative 
consequences for the general price level.

management policy to achieve stability. Generally, monetary policy would remain proactive 
and data driven in achieving a safe, stable, and sound financial system in line with its mandate. 
More importantly, the Bank would continue to monitor the monetary policy environment and 
complement the efforts of the fiscal authority by strengthening fiscal-monetary policy 
coordination to ensure the attainment of stable prices conducive to sustainable growth and 
development. 
The inflation target range is achievable with the adoption of realistic macroeconomic policy

addressing the identified fiscal vulnerabilities will be essential to fostering medium to long-

9.0 RISKS TO THE MEDIUM-TERM OUTLOOK 
Navigating the medium-term global economic landscape requires careful risk assessment to 
ensure fiscal stability. For the Federal Government of Nigeria, a Medium - Term Expenditure 
Framework and Fiscal Strategy Paper (MTEF/FSP) demands a comprehensive analysis of 
how global, regional, and domestic risks are interconnected. 
9.1 Global Economic Trends & Geo-Political Tensions 
The global economy is navigating a period of sustained transformation, defined by persistent 
inflation, aggressive monetary responses, and shifting supply chains against a backdrop of 
heightened geopolitical risk. This challenging environment has moderated global growth, 
which slowed to an average of 3.1% over 2023 - 2024 as rising interest rates and volatile 
energy prices dampened worldwide demand. Looking ahead to 2026 - 2028, growth is 
expected to remain modest, guided by a gradual easing of monetary policy in the US and EU, 
the strategic rebalancing of China's economy, and a powerful shift of capital toward green and 
digital investments. 
However, the path forward is lined with significant uncertainty. Lingering inflationary pressures 
in major economies like the United States and the European Union, combined with the ongoing 
effects of tighter monetary policy, threaten to suppress global demand and slow growth among 
key trade partners. Further supply chain disruptions whether from geopolitical strife, energy 
crises, or climate events could trigger renewed volatility in essential commodity markets, 
particularly for oil, which remains a cornerstone of Nigeria's revenue and foreign exchange 
earnings. These interconnected risks are amplified by the potential for sharp fluctuations in 
exchange and interest rates, which may in turn increase the cost of debt servicing and disrupt 
the flow of international trade. 
 
Sub-Saharan Africa 
The Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) region is projected to sustain moderate growth over the 
medium-term period, but the trajectory will remain fragile and uneven across countries. Debt 
vulnerabilities are expected to persist, with several economies approaching or already in debt 
distress, leaving limited fiscal space to respond to shocks. Inflation is forecast to moderate but 
remain elevated relative to historical averages, partly due to climate induced disruptions to 
food production and volatility in global energy and fertiliser prices. 
Investment recovery may be constrained by tighter global financial conditions, subdued 
investor confidence, and structural bottlenecks in infrastructure and human capital 
development. In addition, heightened climate related risks, including recurrent flooding and 
droughts, pose a growing threat to food security, rural livelihoods, and fiscal stability, while 
governance and security challenges in parts of the region could delay much needed economic 
reforms and weaken capital inflows. 
 
West Africa 
West Africa faces a delicate balance between growth opportunities from natural resource 
exploitation and risks stemming from political fragility and security tensions. Over the medium 
term, ongoing instability in the Sahel, including military takeovers and insurgency activities, 
will likely disrupt regional trade flows, increase security related public spending, and strain 

59 
 
fiscal balances. The region’s commodity dependence will remain a key vulnerability, with 
global price volatility influencing export earnings and fiscal revenues. 
Rising global interest rates could further elevate debt-servicing costs for highly leveraged 
countries, limiting fiscal space for infrastructure and social spending. Persistently high 
inflation, compounded by exchange rate volatility and imported food price shocks, will continue 
to erode household purchasing power and may intensify poverty rates. The risk of social unrest 
remains significant, particularly if economic reforms are perceived as inequitable or if external 
shocks deepen hardship. Political instability in the Sahel, particularly in Niger, Burkina Faso, 
and Mali, continues to disrupt vital regional trade corridors and is compelling neighbouring 
states to divert significant resources toward security spending. 
Furthermore, the planned currency reforms within the WAEMU bloc, involving the replacement 
of the CFA franc with the ECO, could fundamentally alter long-standing monetary coordination 
and introduce new uncertainties. This combination of fragile security conditions and potential 
monetary policy shifts continues to pose substantial risks to the region’s economic stability. 
 
Nigeria 
Nigeria’s medium-term outlook remains highly sensitive to both domestic reforms and external 
conditions. Exchange rate convergence and ongoing subsidy reforms present opportunities to 
restore macroeconomic balance but also carry short-term social costs that will require careful 
management. Fiscal risks will be dominated by elevated debt service obligations, with debt 
service to revenue ratios projected to remain above sustainable thresholds unless revenue 
mobilisation accelerates meaningfully. 
 
Oil sector underperformance remains a critical vulnerability, as production constraints, 
infrastructure decay, and crude theft could limit gains from higher global oil prices. Inflationary 
pressures, driven by supply chain bottlenecks, currency depreciation, and energy costs, may 
continue to challenge monetary and fiscal coordination. Structural weaknesses, including 
infrastructure deficits, a large informal sector, and persistent insecurity in food producing and 
oil-bearing regions threaten to dampen growth potential and investor confidence. Externally, 
Nigeria remains exposed to a stronger U.S. dollar, tightening global liquidity, and geopolitical 
disruptions affecting shipping lanes and energy markets, all of which could heighten exchange 
rate volatility and pressure foreign reserves. 
Against this backdrop, Nigeria’s medium-term fiscal strategy must focus on building resilience 
through conservative macroeconomic assumptions, diversifying revenue sources, and 
reprioritising expenditures toward growth enhancing sectors such as power, transport, and 
human capital. Strengthening public financial management, accelerating the clearance of 
arrears, and developing risk mitigation tools such as FX hedging for debt service and 
contingency buffers for climate and security shocks will be essential to safeguard fiscal 
sustainability and support inclusive growth through 2028.

9.2.3 Energy Transition and Decarbonization Efforts 
The global imperative for environmental sustainability is fundamentally reshaping the oil 
market, introducing significant long-term transition risks. The concerted worldwide push to 
decarbonize economies through stricter emissions regulations, carbon pricing, and ambitious 
renewable energy targets is systematically depressing long-term oil demand forecasts. This 
structural shift is already forcing a strategic re-evaluation within the oil and gas industry, as 
companies and investor nations like Nigeria adapt their investment portfolios and corporate 
strategies to a future increasingly defined by cleaner energy sources. Therefore, the very 
landscape of the oil market is being reconfigured, making the energy transition a dominant 
and enduring risk to hydrocarbon-dependent economies. 
9.3 Exchange Rate Risks 
The significant exchange rate reforms undertaken over the past year, particularly the 
unification of windows and the ensuing convergence between the official and parallel market 
rates, have markedly improved foreign exchange market transparency and laid a crucial 
foundation for stability. This reform has been instrumental in rebuilding foreign exchange 
reserves and bolstering investor confidence. Notwithstanding these improvements, exchange 
rate risk remains a critical factor for Nigeria's economic stability and fiscal planning over the 
medium term (2026-2028). 
 
Looking ahead, the primary risk is the sustainability of this stability. The ability of the Central 
Bank of Nigeria (CBN) to maintain adequate reserve buffers will be tested by substantial 
external debt servicing obligations, ongoing import financing needs, and potential capital flow 
volatility. A depletion of reserves could renew depreciation pressures on the Naira. Such a 
scenario would have immediate fiscal consequences, significantly escalate the domestic cost 
of external debt servicing and crowding out essential capital expenditure. Furthermore, any 
resurgence of volatility would directly fuel imported inflation, erode the real value of 
government revenues, and undermine the investment climate. 
 
Therefore, consolidating the gains of recent reforms and mitigating these risks will require 
sustained policy focus. The priority over the medium term must be to transition from 
stabilization to building structural resilience. This entails aggressively implementing policies 
that boost non-oil exports, attract stable foreign direct investment, and deepen the domestic 
forex market. By reducing the economy's dependence on oil imports and speculative capital 
flows, these measures are essential to shoring up the external sector, managing volatility, and 
enhancing Nigeria's long-term resilience to external shocks. 
9.4 Risks to FGN Revenue Outlook 
The medium-term revenue outlook for the Federal Government of Nigeria (FGN) is subject to 
several risks that could impact fiscal sustainability. Key concerns include the volatility in global 
oil prices, which exposes government revenues to significant fluctuations, alongside persistent 
domestic production challenges that constrain output. These factors could lead to substantial 
revenue shortfalls against projections. Furthermore, the non-oil revenue base continues to 
face structural constraints, including a narrow tax base and compliance gaps, which limit its 
potential contribution. External risks, such as a global economic downturn, could further 
suppress demand and complicate the achievement of revenue targets.

In direct response to these vulnerabilities, the government is proactively advancing a suite of 
strategic reforms to bolster revenue resilience. A central pillar of this strategy is the planned 
amendment to the Petroleum Industry Act (PIA), designed to enhance the fiscal terms and 
regulatory framework of the hydrocarbon sector. This initiative aims to attract critical 
investment, reverse production declines, moderate management fees and ultimately boost oil 
and gas revenue. Concurrently, the full implementation of new tax laws effective from January 
2026 is critical to strengthening domestic revenue mobilization. These laws are designed to 
widen the tax base, improve compliance, and create a more efficient and predictable tax 
system. 
These targeted interventions in the hydrocarbon and fiscal regimes are complemented by 
ongoing efforts to diversify the economy and rationalize public expenditure. The combined 
focus on optimizing oil sector earnings through the PIA amendment and fortifying non-oil 
collections via the new tax framework provides a balanced and robust approach to revenue 
mobilization. This comprehensive strategy is designed to mitigate exposure to external 
shocks, reduce fiscal deficits, and foster greater fiscal stability over the medium term. 
 
9.5 Sensitivity of Budget aggregates to Macro-economic 
conditions 
Nigeria's fiscal aggregates over the medium term will be highly contingent on the performance 
of key macroeconomic variables. The budget faces significant exposure to oil price volatility, 
exchange rate fluctuations, and domestic inflationary pressures. A decline in oil prices would 
directly impair revenue collection. Inflation, meanwhile, risks eroding the real value of revenue 
and increasing program implementation costs. 
Furthermore, the fiscal outlook is sensitive to debt servicing requirements and economic 
growth rates. Rising debt service costs, susceptible to interest rate changes, may crowd out 
vital capital expenditure. Additionally, revenue forecasts are predicated on growth 
assumptions, with any shortfall likely to result in budgetary underperformance. 
In response, the Government's medium-term policy framework will focus on fostering 
economic diversification, strengthening non-oil revenue streams, and the new tax law 
expected to kick in from 2026 will serve as implementing shock absorbing policies to safeguard 
fiscal sustainability.
